## Abstract

This article presents albedo measurements of snow and glacier ice at Vatnajökull (Iceland) and the Kangerlussuaq transect (Greenland). Radiative fluxes were measured in the broadband and in four narrowbands, namely, Thematic Mapper (TM) Bands 2 and 4, and Advanced Very High Resolution Radiometer (AVHRR) Bands 1 and 2. The incoming fluxes were measured near the ground and the outgoing fluxes from a helicopter. Extracts of the data collected over snow, ice, supraglacial moraines, supraglacial lakes, and tundra are discussed. Using the data sets from Iceland and Greenland, and data sets with entirely ground-based albedo measurements from the Morteratschgletscher (Switzerland) and Scharffenbergbotnen (Antarctica), new equations for narrowband-to-broadband (NTB) conversion were developed. They have a residual standard deviation of 0.011 for TM and 0.008 for AVHRR and can be applied without having to classify the surface. The helicopter data are also used to develop criteria for distinguishing different types of surfaces, which are needed for the application of Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Functions (BRDFs). Snow can be distinguished from ice by defining a threshold for a single narrowband albedo. The ratio of the albedos in TM4 and TM2, and to a lesser extent, the ratio of the albedos in AVHRR2 and AVHRR1, may serve as a proxy for the surface characteristics of glacier ice in terms of concentrations of water, debris, and dust.

## Introduction

The surface albedo is the fraction of the incoming solar radiation that is reflected by the surface of the Earth. On glaciers and ice sheets, variations in surface albedo have a large effect on the amount of melt because during the melt season absorption of solar radiation generally is the dominant source of energy Greuell & Genthon, in press, Hock, 1998. However, at present, simulations with numerical models of temporal and spatial variations in snow and ice albedo are inaccurate because there is insufficient knowledge about these variations. Though the effects of grain size and shape on the albedo of snow are reasonably well understood (Brun, David, Sudul, & Brunot, 1992), we know little about variations in the albedo of glacier ice and snow caused by variations in the amounts of debris, dust, and water on and near the surface. Ground-based measurements may provide some of the necessary data, notably on temporal variations at specific locations, but provide little information on spatial variations because they represent only small areas of often highly inhomogeneous surfaces. Fortunately, the albedo can be retrieved from satellite images, which cover the entire surface of the Earth. Thus, satellite retrieval of the surface albedo of glaciers and ice sheets promises to become a valuable contribution to the improvement of melt simulations by numerical models. Using satellite data, maps of the surface albedo were produced for the Greenland ice sheet by Greuell and Knap (2000) and Stroeve, Nolin, and Steffen (1997), for Vatnajökull by Reijmer, Knap, and Oerlemans (1999), and for the Haut Glacier d'Arolla (Switzerland) by Knap, Brock, Oerlemans, and Willis (1999).

The problem with satellite sensors is that they do not directly measure the surface albedo. Satellite sensors receive signals representing radiances (radiation from a particular direction) in narrowbands (width up to a few hundred nanometers) at the top of the atmosphere. These signals must be converted into the surface albedo, which is the ratio of two fluxes integrated over the solar spectrum at the surface. For conversion of the satellite signals into the surface albedo, the following steps must be made:

  * •

computing the incoming narrowband radiative fluxes at the top of the atmosphere;

  * •

converting the satellite signals into narrowband radiances by means of the calibration coefficients for the satellite sensors;

  * •

applying an atmospheric correction;

  * •

correcting the incoming fluxes for the orientation of the surface;

  * •

applying a correction for anisotropic reflection by the surface. The directional distribution of the reflected radiation is described by so-called Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Functions (BRDFs).

The results of these five steps are narrowband surface albedos. The final step of the retrieval method then is:

⋅ converting the narrowband surface albedos into the “broadband surface albedo,” a term used in satellite meteorology for the meteorological term “surface albedo,” which includes all of the solar radiation. To determine the broadband albedo, narrowband-to-broadband conversion (NTB conversion) is indispensable because the albedos of snow and glacier ice vary with wavelength (Fig. 1).

The main topic of this paper is NTB conversion. More specifically, we deal with the conversion of narrowband surface albedos in Landsat Thematic Mapper (TM) Bands 2 (520–600 nm) and 4 (760–900 nm), and in NOAA Advanced Very High Resolution Radiometer (AVHRR) Bands 1 (580–680 nm) and 2 (730–1100 nm). In Section 2, we discuss previous studies on NTB conversion of TM and AVHRR albedos of snow and ice. In Section 3, we describe recent field experiments. We carried out narrowband and broadband albedo measurements from a helicopter during flights over Vatnajökull (Iceland) and the Kangerlussuaq transect (K-transect), located on the western part of the Greenland ice sheet at 67°N (Fig. 2). We chose to do these measurements from aircraft, because it covers much larger areas, compared to ground-based measurements. Section 3.2 describes the properties and calibration of the narrowband and broadband pyranometers used for the measurements. Section 3.3 covers the experimental set-up and strategy and the methods employed to correct the raw data. We discuss surface conditions during the flights in the same section and show extracts of the measured albedos for selected flight legs. Section 3.4 considers ground-based measurements on the Morteratschgletscher (Switzerland) and in Scharffenbergbotnen (Antarctica), by Knap, Reijmer, and Oerlemans (1999) and Reijmer, Bintanja, and Greuell (2001), respectively, performed with the same pyranometers as used for the helicopter measurements. The albedo measurements from Iceland, Greenland, the Alps, and Antarctica together cover a wide range of surface conditions. In Section 4, we use the four data sets to determine equations for NTB conversion for snow and ice.

Two questions relevant to the conversion of satellite data into surface albedos of snow and ice are (a) whether it is possible to discriminate between snow and ice in the satellite data, and (b) whether it is possible to distinguish glacier ice with debris and dust on the surface from glacier ice with water on the surface. For NTB conversion, such a classification seems irrelevant because the equations for NTB conversion can be applied without having to classify the surface. Classification does matter, however, when a correction for anisotropic reflection at the surface is made. BRDFs of snow (Nolin & Liang, 2000) differ from those of ice (Greuell & De Ruyter de Wildt, 1999), and BRDFs of glacier ice with debris and dust on the surface differ from BRDFs of glacier ice with water on the surface. In Section 5, we use our Greenland and Iceland data to try to develop criteria for the separation of these types of glacier surface.

## Abstract

The MODIS instrument provides major advances in moderate resolution earth observation. Improved spatial resolution for land observation at 250 and 500 m and improved spectral band placement provide new remote sensing opportunities. NASA has invested in the development of improved algorithms for MODIS, which will provide new data sets for global change research. Surface reflectance is one of the key products from MODIS and is used in developing several higher-order land products. The surface reflectance algorithm builds on the heritage of the Advanced Very High Resolution Radiometer (AVHRR) and SeaWiFS algorithms, taking advantage of the new sensing capabilities of MODIS. Atmospheric correction by the removal of water vapor and aerosol effects provides improvements over previous coarse resolution products and the basis for a new time-series, which will extend through to the NPOESS generation imagers.
