# Airborne multispectral data for quantifying leaf area index, nitrogen concentration, and photosynthetic efficiency in agriculture

## Abstract

Airborne multispectral data were acquired by the Compact Airborne Spectral Imager (CASI) for an agricultural area in Denmark with the purpose of quantifying vegetation amount and variations in the physiological status of the vegetation. Spectral reflectances, vegetation indices, and red edge positions were calculated on the basis of the CASI data and compared to field measurements of green leaf area index (LAI; L) and canopy nitrogen concentrations (N_{c}) sampled at 16 sites. Because of the variety of the samples with respect to vegetation type, leaf age, and phenological developmental stage, the data of L and N_{c} were uncorrelated. The scattering effect of leaves effectuated a robust linear relationship between L and near-infrared (NIR) reflectance (r=.93), whereas the N_{c} (vegetative period) was significantly correlated with the spectral reflectance in the green (r=−.88) and far-red wavebands (r=−.94). The correlations between vegetation indices and L were also important, in particular, for the enhanced vegetation index (EVI; r=.88), whereas the red edge position correlated less significantly with N_{c} (r=.78). Assuming L and N_{c} to be responsible for most of the spatial variability in the CO_{2} assimilation rates, remote sensing-based maps of these variables were produced for use in a coupled sun/shade photosynthesis/transpiration model. The predicted rates of net photosynthesis and transpiration compared reasonably with eddy covariance measurements of CO_{2} and water vapour fluxes recorded at four different crop fields. The results allowed evaluation of the spatial variations in the photosynthetic light, nitrogen, and water use efficiencies. While photosynthesis was linearly related to the transpiration, the light use efficiency (LUE) was found to be dependent on nitrogen concentrations.


## Introduction

Remote sensing data can be used to predict photosynthetic rates and biomass production using methodologies relating spectral vegetation indices to light absorption (i.e. Hanan et al., 1995, Prince, 1991, Waring et al., 1995) or to maximum photosynthetic capacities Carter, 1998, Steven et al., 1983, Yoder & Waring, 1994, which are also closely related to leaf chlorophyll and nitrogen contents. Because canopy light absorption is tightly related to the green leaf area index (LAI) and because the photosynthetic pigments (mainly chlorophylls) of green leaves are responsible for the absorption of photosynthetically active radiation (PAR), a high degree of intercorrelation between canopy photosynthetic rates, LAI, absorbed PAR, and chlorophyll concentrations is normal in natural ecosystems (i.e. Blackburn, 1998, Pierce et al., 1994, Pinar & Curran, 1996). In managed systems, however, high leaf photosynthetic capacities during the initial growth phases (at low LAI) and low photosynthetic capacities prevailing during senescence (also at low LAI) cause LAI and leaf photosynthetic capacity to be uncorrelated. In order to predict correctly the photosynthetic rates of such canopies, it is necessary to assess both the light absorption capability and the leaf photosynthetic capacity.

The consideration of both light absorption and photosynthetic capacity in remote sensing-based photosynthesis modeling was introduced by Sellers, Berry, Collatz, Field, and Hall (1992). Using a remote sensing-based light use factor (the vegetation index), standard meteorological data, and the presetting of photosynthetic parameters, canopy photosynthesis was predicted based on mechanistic leaf photosynthesis equations. The major problem remained the selection of the appropriate value to use for the maximum catalytic capacity of Rubisco (V_{m}). Because of the close relationship between leaf nitrogen and V_{m}, estimates of leaf nitrogen concentrations are important for this parameterization (Nijs, Behaeghe, & Impens, 1995). The present paper attempts to demonstrate the feasibility of remote sensing-based estimates of LAI and nitrogen concentrations in photosynthesis modeling.

While several leaf scale studies confirm the use of spectral reflectance data to predict nitrogen and chlorophyll concentrations, the viability of remote sensing-based techniques to estimate LAI and the absorbed PAR is established through canopy scale studies (see review in Sellers, 1989). In particular, the red edge position (i.e. Gitelson, Merzylak, & Lichtenthaler, 1996) and the spectral reflectance in the chlorophyll maximum reflectance bands (the green and far-red regions) are strongly related to the chlorophyll and nitrogen contents of leaves (i.e. Gitelson et al., 1996a, Lichtenthaler et al., 1996). The canopy reflectance in the green and far-red regions is also sensitive to variations in chlorophyll concentrations (i.e. Yoder & Pettigrew-Crosby, 1995). However, because measurements of canopy reflectance are also influenced by LAI, background (soil) reflectance, sun/view angles, canopy geometry, and atmospheric conditions (for air/spaceborne remote sensing), the equations derived from leaf-level measurements are not valid at the canopy scale (Yoder & Pettigrew-Crosby, 1995). In order to factor out variability due to causes other than variations in chlorophyll concentrations, the use of simple reflectance ratios or indices is recommended (i.e. Broge & LeBlanc, 2001, Gitelson et al., 1996a). Another concern related to canopy scale studies is that many experiments relating canopy reflectance and chlorophyll concentrations did not achieve full independency between their data on leaf chemistry and LAI/biomass Blackburn, 1998, Blackburn & Steele, 1999, Pinar & Curran, 1996, Yoder & Pettigrew-Crosby, 1995. This makes it difficult to detect the cause of the observed relationships between the spectral data and the vegetation features (Yoder & Pettigrew-Crosby, 1995), and it could lead to noncausal relationships between leaf properties and canopy reflectance (Blackburn & Steele, 1999). Yoder and Waring (1994) were, however, able to control variations of chlorophyll concentrations and LAI in their experiment and found that such changes had nearly independent effects on the spectral reflectance. While reflectance in the green band was very sensitive to chlorophyll concentrations, variations of LAI influenced mainly the near-infrared (NIR) reflectance.

In the present paper, the feasibility of airborne narrow-band reflectance data to predict independent quantities of LAI and nitrogen concentration are presented. The results are used to produce maps of LAI and vegetation nitrogen concentration (N_{c}) which are applied in a mechanistic photosynthesis model (de Pury & Farquhar, 1997). The modelled CO_{2} assimilation rates are validated using measured canopy fluxes of CO_{2} which have been corrected for soil respiration.

Many algorithms have been developed for the remote estimation of biophysical characteristics of vegetation, in terms of combinations of spectral bands, derivatives of reflectance spectra, neural networks, inversion of radiative transfer models, and several multi-spectral statistical approaches. However, the most widespread type of algorithm used is the mathematical combination of visible and near-infrared reflectance bands, in the form of spectral vegetation indices. Applications of such vegetation indices have ranged from leaves to the entire globe, but in many instances, their applicability is specific to species, vegetation types or local conditions. The general objective of this study is to evaluate different vegetation indices for the remote estimation of the green leaf area index (Green LAI) of two crop types (maize and soybean) with contrasting canopy architectures and leaf structures. Among the indices tested, the chlorophyll Indices (the CI_{Green}, the CI_{Red-edge} and the MERIS Terrestrial Chlorophyll Index, MTCI) exhibited strong and significant linear relationships with Green LAI, and thus were sensitive across the entire range of Green LAI evaluated (i.e., 0.0 to more than 6.0 m^{2}/m^{2}). However, the CI_{Red-edge} was the only index insensitive to crop type and produced the most accurate estimations of Green LAI in both crops (RMSE = 0.577 m^{2}/m^{2}). These results were obtained using data acquired with close range sensors (i.e., field spectroradiometers mounted 6 m above the canopy) and an aircraft-mounted hyperspectral imaging spectroradiometer (AISA). As the CI_{Red-edge} also exhibited low sensitivity to soil background effects, it constitutes a simple, yet robust tool for the remote and synoptic estimation of Green LAI. Algorithms based on this index may not require re-parameterization when applied to crops with different canopy architectures and leaf structures, but further studies are required for assessing its applicability in other vegetation types (e.g., forests, grasslands).

Net ecosystem exchange (NEE) of CO_{2} between the atmosphere and forest ecosystems is determined by gross primary production (GPP) of vegetation and ecosystem respiration. CO_{2} flux measurements at individual CO_{2} eddy flux sites provide valuable information on the seasonal dynamics of GPP. In this paper, we developed and validated the satellite-based Vegetation Photosynthesis Model (VPM), using site-specific CO_{2} flux and climate data from a temperate deciduous broadleaf forest at Harvard Forest, Massachusetts, USA. The VPM model is built upon the conceptual partitioning of photosynthetically active vegetation and non-photosynthetic vegetation (NPV) within the leaf and canopy. It estimates GPP, using satellite-derived Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI), Land Surface Water Index (LSWI), air temperature and photosynthetically active radiation (PAR). Multi-year (1998–2001) data analyses have shown that EVI had a stronger linear relationship with GPP than did the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI). Two simulations of the VPM model were conducted, using vegetation indices from the VEGETATION (VGT) sensor onboard the SPOT-4 satellite and the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) sensor onboard the Terra satellite. The predicted GPP values agreed reasonably well with observed GPP of the deciduous broadleaf forest at Harvard Forest, Massachusetts. This study highlighted the biophysical performance of improved vegetation indices in relation to GPP and demonstrated the potential of the VPM model for scaling-up of GPP of deciduous broadleaf forests.

## Introduction

A reliable and accurate forest inventory is required to develop and modify present land use and silvicultural practices in Pacific Northwest forests. Presently, these forests are a heterogeneous mosaic of remaining mature forests, clearcuts, new plantations, and second-growth stands in areas that formerly were dominated by extensive old-growth forests and younger forests resulting from fire disturbance (Spies & Franklin, 1988). This heterogeneity is further increased by the different rates of regrowth within the clearcut areas influenced by many factors such as soil condition, sun exposure, moisture availability, replanting history, and elevation. Traditionally, forest inventories have been made using high-resolution aerial photography and local field observations, both of which are time-consuming and resource-intensive. The forest characteristics on which inventories are based include age, species, tree diameter at breast height, spacing, state of health, canopy closure, tree height, and stand size. Although these characteristics are difficult to measure directly with current remote sensing instruments, the synoptic view of satellites makes remote sensing an attractive tool to complement and extend field studies of forests.

Combinations of physical forest characteristics affect the spectral attributes of the canopy crown at the pixel scale (tens of meters). For remote sensing to be useful in forest mapping, it is necessary either to recover critical forest parameters by inversion of spectral measurements of the canopy, or to establish new mapping criteria on the basis of spectral data directly. Spectral mixture analysis (SMA) is an inversion approach to mapping in which forests are viewed as mixtures of basic components: green foliage, tree bark and stems, soil, and the unresolved (subpixel) shadows cast by the vegetation. Each of these endmembers is defined spectrally, but has a physical equivalent in the scene itself (“physical endmember”). For example, Sabol et al. (1995) have shown that subpixel shadows in images are related to canopy roughness, and hence can be used as the basis for seral stage identification. In SMA, fractional amounts of the spectral endmembers are estimated through inversion of a mixing model. For this inversion to be effective, it is necessary to calibrate the remotely sensed radiance measurements to reflectance by relating image spectra to those made in the laboratory of similar materials. In order to relate the endmember fractions to quantitative amounts of the physical equivalents in the scene, it is necessary to perform a second level of calibration, based on field measurement of the physical endmembers. Connection to conventionally measured forest parameters likewise needs to be established by field study. This lengthy chain of calibrations and field measurement is costly and diminishes the time-saving advantages and the reliability of remote-sensing analysis. Therefore, as the connections between image data and field measurements are understood, it may become possible to reduce the length of this chain and analyze forests in terms of their spectral components directly. A key issue in remote sensing research is the utility, and specifically the degree of accuracy and precision, of forest maps made this way. Our study focuses on these questions using SMA for the identification and mapping of forest structural stage and stand age based on the characteristic patterns in the amounts of spectral endmembers calculated from a Landsat Thematic Mapper (TM) image of a forest in the Cascade Mountain Range of southern Washington, USA.
